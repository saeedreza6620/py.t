           Abstract. Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) copolymer (PLGA) microspheres are crucial in
           drug delivery due to their ability to provide controlled release of a wide range of ther-
           apeutic agents while minimizing side effects and enhancing biocompatibility. In this
           paper, a mathematical model is proposed and numerically studied for the drug release
           process in PLGA microspheres. A three-dimensional diffusion model is constructed
           based on Fick’s law, being different from the traditional one-dimensional model in the
           past and fitting the configuration from the drug delivery system, while an h-adaptive
           finite element method is designed and realized using a C++ library AFEPack for nu-
           merical solutions of the proposed model. A notable feature is that the h-adaptivity
           makes the proposed model and numerical method a quality tool for studying con-
           cerned phenomena such as initial bursts in drug delivery. In numerical experiments,
           besides demonstrating the convergence of the numerical method, a case on proges-
           terone release from PLGA is also studied using the proposed model, from which the
           potential of the model and the method towards the practical application is shown suc-
           cessfully.

1     Introduction
Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is the copolymer first approved by the FDA in Jan-
uary 1989 [1]. Because of its superb biodegradability and biocompatibility, it has been
popularly researched as a biological material in drug delivery systems [2, 3]. By slowly
releasing drug molecules over an extensive period starting from a single administration,
these systems can control the drug concentrations between the target scopes, decrease
side effects caused by extraordinary concentrations and repeated administrations, and
enhance patient compliance in contrast with traditional therapies [4, 5]. Intending to ob-
tain a required dosage depending on the type of medication, the degradation time of the
polymer matrix can be controlled by modifying some parameter values, such as molecu-
lar weight of the polymer, drug concentration, and proportion of lactide to glycolide [6,7].
In spite of these benefits, the characterization and design of PLGA mainly rely on plenty
of trial experiments and error modification, and it is still being explored that the interac-
tion among complicated processes that influence the drug release [8].
    With the development of drug delivery systems, it is critical to optimize therapeu-
tic effects by accurately controlling the release rate of drugs. Mathematical modeling
provides a powerful tool for predicting and optimizing drug release kinetics, as they can
quantitatively demonstrate the complicated interactions between drug properties, design
of the delivery system, and environmental factors [9]. By combining experimental data
with mathematical models, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms will be gained by
underlying drug release and more efficient and effective delivery systems [10]. Thus, the
integration of mathematical modeling with drug release research is essential for advanc-
ing drug delivery technology and improving the prognosis of patients.
    In order to reduce the multitudinous experiments predicting the unknown trial re-
sults, establishing a suitable mathematical model is a practical approach to address the
problem. With the inspiration from heat conduction, Fick first founded the diffusion
equation in 1855. The main idea of this equation is that the transition rate of diffusive
material through per unit cross-sectional area proportionate to the concentration gradi-
ent with the measurement from perpendicular to the cross-section [11]. In 1975, Crank
collected the previous mathematical theories of diffusion, including the discussion of the
solution and the numerical experiments [12]. Since 2000, a common method has built
a relevance between the effective drug diffusivity and molecular weight with exponen-
tial decline, which is on account of the pseudo-first-order degradation kinetics [13–15].
Ford et al. (2011) developed a reaction-diffusion model to generate quadratic autocat-
alytic degradation of the matrix of polymer and evolution in the pore structure [16].
Casalini et al. and Busatto et al. considered the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis mechanism
to consist of the polymer degradation process as a system of partial differential equa-
tions(PDEs), the model also takes the autocatalytic influence into consideration [17–19].
Recently, the machine learning method has also been utilized for the estimation of effec-
tive diffusion coefficients in a one-dimensional diffusion-erosion model [20]. Notwith-
standing the methodology of building the mathematical model has become increasingly

sophisticated in drug release from the polymeric matrix, the traditional equations and
simulation results are focused on the one-dimensional case, which lacks multidimen-
sional considerations and multi-level observations.
     The aim of this paper is to construct a three-dimensional diffusion model and de-
velop the corresponding numerical methods, for the exploration of the mechanism of the
drug release from PLGA-based microparticles. Mathematically, to simplify the model,
the region of the microsphere is regarded as a uniform sphere that is bounded. The di-
mensionless form is utilized to modify the diffusion model and generalize the analysis
of the delivery process. The motivation for focusing on the diffusion model stems from
its fundamental role in accurately capturing the mechanisms of drug transport within the
microsphere. Diffusion is a primary driving force in the release of drugs from solid matri-
ces, particularly in polymeric systems where the drug must migrate through the polymer
network before reaching the surrounding medium. By employing a diffusion-based strat-
egy, the concentration gradients can be effectively accounted for with the development of
the drug released, which is critical for predicting the release kinetics. In contrast to other
models, such as swelling models, the diffusion model allows for a more precise analysis
of the temporal changes in drug concentration. This is particularly significant in applica-
tions where controlled release is desired, as it provides insights into how varying factors,
such as the size of the microsphere, the solubility of the drug, and the properties of the
polymer matrix, affect the overall release profile. Furthermore, the three-dimensional
diffusion equation enables us to simulate the spatial distribution of the drug within the
microsphere over time. This capability is essential for understanding how the drug is
released in a non-uniform manner, this can occur due to factors like uneven drug dis-
tribution or variations in polymer density. By focusing on this model, the optimization
of drug formulations and therapeutic outcomes can be enhanced, thereby ensuring that
drugs are delivered at the right rate and concentration to achieve maximum efficacy. Nu-
merically, the governing equation in the polymeric matrix is discretized in a matrix for-
mulation system. After this process, the corresponding algorithms can be developed for
solving the proposed model in AFEPack [21], which is a free and flexible computer pro-
gram implemented in C++ [22, 23]. This package is a set of tools and libraries with FEM
for solving PDEs. The design of the error indicator is calculated for each grid unit based
on the distance from two specific points, where the indicator value is set to its volume if
the distance to both points is less than a specified threshold. The AFEM will be utilized
to show the detailed regions where the concentration is higher than its surroundings.
The simulation results provide insights into the potential singularity caused by the initial
burst phenomenon, which refers to the dramatic release of the drug at the beginning of
the delivery process. To elucidate this phenomenon, we first construct an array consist-
ing of grid elements, ensuring that each element corresponds to a specific region within
the microsphere. This array is designed to store the concentration values for each grid
element, allowing us to analyze the concentration gradients effectively. Next, we calcu-
late the concentration gradients for each grid element and its neighboring elements. By
determining the difference between these gradients, the concentration can be assessed
900                   H. Chen, G. Hu and D. Ouyang / Adv. Appl. Math. Mech., 18 (2026), pp. 897-922


and how changes from one element to another. This difference is then multiplied by the
corresponding normal vector to create an indicator that reflects the rate and direction of
drug release. This detailed approach not only helps in quantifying the initial burst but
also enhances our understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving drug release in
the microsphere. By analyzing these indicators, valuable insights can be gained into op-
timizing drug delivery systems for improved therapeutic efficacy. The computational
geometry is generated by Gmsh, an open-source software to create 3D and 2D meshes
for numerical simulations and finite element analysis [24]. In addition, the numerical re-
sults for all discretizing points can handle the complicated regions (like the microsphere)
and understand data from different sources, including simulations, measurements, and
experiments [25]. The convergence and stability analysis are designed to verify the feasi-
bility of this standard model. Also, the comparison of the different diffusion coefficients
in various directions is developed to research the influence of the velocity of drug release
to the outer atmosphere. Furthermore, the results of the numerical simulation can quali-
tatively fit the experimental data, which is optimized by polynomial fitting methodology.
This research can be further utilized on other models, including the oral extended-release
hydrophilic matrix tablets, for further research.
    The rest of this paper is demonstrated as follows. In Section 2, we introduced the
model geometry and the government equation. Then, a well-designed indicator in the
Adaptive finite element and discretizing form is proposed in Section 3. In section 4, the
optimization method is derived from the Newton method. In Section 5, we present the
numerical results and the comparison with the experimental data.

4    The reproduction of diffusion coefficient from experimental
     data

The precise reproduction of the diffusion coefficient from experimental data is funda-
mental for the validation and refinement of diffusion models that have shown substan-
tial predictive accuracy. While existing models effectively encapsulate the underlying
mechanisms of diffusion, their integration with empirical data is essential for enhancing
their robustness and applicability in real-world scenarios. In pursuit of this objective,
we have implemented advanced optimization techniques to systematically adjust model
parameters. This iterative refinement not only facilitates a more accurate alignment be-
tween theoretical predictions and experimental results but also contributes to a deeper
understanding of diffusion phenomena. By bridging the gap between model simulations
and empirical observations, we aim to enhance the predictive power of diffusion models,
thereby advancing their utility across various scientific and engineering disciplines.

4.1   The optimization problem
In this optimization process, we need to determine the two crucial parameters through
experiments to achieve the desired effects. However, conducting experiments directly
is often expensive and time-consuming. A more effective approach is to obtain a small
amount of experimental data, after using polynomial fitting to get the relationship be-
tween the parameters, and then perform numerical simulations to optimize the appro-
priate parameter combination based on this relationship. Specifically, we first randomly
try the parameters of diffusion coefficient D and radius r to the numerical experiment
and obtain an initial result of D and r. However, we find that this set of data cannot fully
meet the requirements. After this, the polynomial fitting method can be utilized to estab-
lish a relationship model with 30 testing data between D and r. Then the minimum value
of data in each group can be calculated, and the update D and r will be used to resolve
the governing equation, where the new numerical simulations with the optimization pa-
rameters can obtain more ideal effects.

5     Numerical experiments

5.2   Results for mesh adaptivity

Based on the diffusion properties of the microsphere, it is observed that the drug con-
centration within the interior region is significantly higher than that at the surface. This
characteristic allows the microsphere to maintain a prolonged drug release over an ex-
tended period [41]. In this subsection, we employ the AFEM to effectively reduce the
computational cost while simultaneously improving the computational efficiency specif-
ically in the interior part of the microsphere (see Fig. 5). By focusing on this area, more
accurate simulations can be achieved without unnecessary computational overhead. To
facilitate this process, we define a steady and elevated concentration within the central
region of the microsphere. The error indicator for each finite element is then calculated
using the gradient and unit vector associated with the drug concentration distribution.
This can be an effective strategy to study the influence of the initial burst since the regions
experiencing higher indicator values may indicate areas where the initial burst effect is
more pronounced. Moreover, the new L2 norm of error is compared with the refined one
and theoretical result, as shown in Fig. 6.

5.3   Variable diffusion coefficient
In this part, the different diffusion coefficients are set to control the drug release. Fol-
lowing the Z coordinate axis, D is equal to 50 when the plane is Z > 0.5 and D is equal
to 0.5 when Z < 0.5. The results are shown in Fig. 7 with time steps. By examining the
figure, it is clear that the drug concentration in the lower regions of the Z coordinate axis
still exists, despite the use of a smaller diffusion coefficient. It indicates that the smaller
diffusion coefficient results in a slower rate of drug diffusion, which in turn leads to a
longer duration of drug release.


5.4   The comparison between numerical results and experimental data
In the previous research [42], the quantity of drug released details with the time was de-
termined by the function of Mt /M∞ , and the details of deduction in the spherical regions
are in the Appendix.
    In our numerical model, the relevant parameter to describe the drug released with
time can be proposed by 1 − C̃h,max , where the C̃h,max represents the maximum value of
concentration obtained by the simulated results at the specific moment. Out of the con-
sideration of consistency in our algorithm, the simulated values of C̃h will only appear at
the internal regions of all tetrahedrons. The parameter value 1 − C̃h can demonstrate the
amount of drug release in every tetrahedron. Therefore, with the vanishment of the high-
est concentration in the specific tetrahedron (that is C̃h,max = 0), the release process is con-
sidered to be finished. In Fig. 8, the diffusion coefficient ranges from D = 4.6 × 10−14 cm2 /s
to D = 2 × 10−12 cm2 /s, and the experimental data are similar with a range from 7.2µm to
53µm [26], which is represented by the red triangles. The numerical results of the initial
mesh are shown with the green sign of the cross, and the refined mesh indicated by the
blue hollow square is closer to the experimental data from the detailed diagram, which
means the refined mesh can optimize the simulated results. In order to fit the experimen-
