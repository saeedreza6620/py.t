   Drug diffusion from a microsphere-COMSOL simulation
What's the duration required for a medication to disappear from a microsphere?
Biodegradable microsphere-based drug delivery methods have been thoroughly studied. By
administering therapy at a regulated speed over an extended period, these devices can minimize
the frequency of administration, maintain and stabilize the medicine, and enhance patient
compliance. Drug delivery applications use biodegradable polymeric microspheres and are
emerging as a major means of delivering medicinal medicines. The benefits of microspheres
include the protection of encapsulated medications, the convenience of noninvasive injectable
delivery, and prolonged drug release over hours, months, or even years. Predicting the release
performance of a drug delivery device is a major difficulty in biological science and
pharmaceutics.
To simulate the question "How long does it take for a drug to deplete from a microsphere?" using
COMSOL simulation, the following conditions must be met: the external liquid medium is well
mixed; the microsphere has spherical symmetry; the drug is evenly distributed throughout the drug
capsule, or, in other words, at the initial time point, the drug has well diffused throughout the entire
microsphere such that the concentration in the capsule is constant; and there is no membrane layer
or anything similar that could cause an increase or decrease in diffusivity throughout or along the
radius of the sphere (constant diffusivity throughout the capsule).
A circle and 2700 are selected as the geometry and rotation angles, respectively.

The physics used in this simulation was chosen to be the transport of diluted organisms. Diffusion
of a medication with a modest molecular weight was taken into account here. Transporting diluted
species is hence appropriate. The question, "How long will this drug take to diffuse out of this
microsphere?" needed to be answered in terms of time. Thus, the study's time-dependent model
was chosen. The microsphere can be defined by a number of factors.


1. Diffusion coefficient (D)-0.01 µm2/s
2. Radius of the capsule (R)- 100 µm
3. Initial concentration of the drug (C0)- 100 mol/m3
There was an axial symmetry across the R=0 axis. There was no flux boundary condition. So, in
this case, the drugs will not even be able to diffuse out of the microsphere. So, the boundary
conditions were updated. It was important to get the constant boundary condition at the interface.

Examining drug diffusion outside of the microsphere was the goal of the simulation. The starting
time of the time-dependent study was set to 0, and the stop time was set to 24 hours. This model
allows one to watch how the diffusion happens over a day. Consequently, a high drug
concentration within the microsphere and a diminishing concentration as it exits through the
surface are visible.
